🧠12 things your brain does that you never noticed

1 · HEBB’S RULE

Why doing something twice makes the third time easier

Neurons that fire together, wire together.
When two neurons are repeatedly active at the same time, the synapse between them becomes more efficient (long-term potentiation, LTP): more receptors, more neurotransmitter, sometimes new contact points. That’s why the lines get thicker every time you do an activity.

2 · MYELIN

Your nerves are getting a highway upgrade

Signals can speed up to over 100 m/s.
Frequently used nerve pathways get wrapped in more myelin – an insulating layer. The signal then jumps from gap to gap (the “nodes of Ranvier”) instead of crawling along – faster and more reliable. You can watch it jump in the synapse zoom.
Tap to flip 🃏
🤖
3 · THE HANDOVER

The day your brain stops asking for permission

From boss to autopilot.
New behavior is controlled consciously by the prefrontal cortex – which is effortful. With every repetition the striatum (basal ganglia) takes over more. In the end a single cue is enough and the routine runs almost by itself.

🔁
4 · THE LOOP

The loop hiding behind every habit you have

Cue → routine → reward.
Dopamine tags what was worth it. The faster and more reliably the reward comes, the faster the habit forms. That’s why scrolling becomes a habit so much faster than running. A fixed cue (“after breakfast”) makes the loop reliable.
Tap to flip 🃏
😴
5 · SLEEP

You keep learning while you sleep

Your brain replays your day – in fast-forward.
During deep sleep the hippocampus “replays” the day’s activity patterns in short, compressed bursts (sharp-wave ripples) and passes them on to the cortex – consolidation. In the game, half of each day’s progress only counts after a good night’s sleep – staying up late costs you.
Tap to flip 🃏
📅
6 · HOW LONG?

Forget 21 days. Try 66.

The famous “21 days” is a myth.
In a study from University College London (Lally et al., 2009) it took a median of 66 days for a new behavior to become automatic – anywhere from 18 to 254 days depending on the person and the behavior. Missing a single day made hardly any difference.

7 · USE IT OR LOSE IT

Old habits never really die

Weaker – but never gone.
Connections that go unused slowly weaken (synaptic pruning). Old habits rarely disappear completely, though – that’s why relapses are so easy. In the game, a path never drops below 40% of its peak strength.

8 · BREAKING A HABIT

Every “no” rewires you too

Resisting is training.
Every time you notice an urge and don’t follow it, the PFC practices top-down control and the old loop gets a little weaker. It’s hard at first and gets easier – just like building a habit.
Tap to flip 🃏
📱
9 · DOPAMINE MOVES

Why just seeing your phone makes you want it

Dopamine moves from the reward to the cue.
At first, dopamine neurons fire when the reward arrives. As you learn, the response shifts to the cue that predicts the reward (Schultz’ “reward prediction error”). That’s why just seeing your phone can create craving – before you’ve decided anything.

10 · CONNECTOME

You have a wiring diagram – and it’s changing

Every habit redraws the map.
A connectome is a complete map of which neurons connect to which. It doesn’t stay fixed: every habit you build or break slowly reshapes your own connectome. Hubs – regions with many strong links – shape how information flows.

🧗
11 · THE 3 PHASES

Every habit climbs the same 3 stages

Initiation → learning → stability.
Habit researchers describe three phases: deciding to start, repeating it in the same context while automaticity grows, and finally a stable habit that runs with little effort. The table shows which phase each habit is in.

12 · STRATEGIES

4 tricks that beat willpower

Small, fixed, stacked, friction.
Start tiny (repetition matters more than size). Use a fixed cue and stack new habits onto old ones. Design your environment (friction for bad habits). Never miss twice. All of these are in the game.

ℹ️ About the “energy” bar (simplified) The idea that willpower is a battery that runs empty (“ego depletion”) is debated in psychology – many studies failed to replicate it. What is well established: new behaviors feel effortful because they need conscious control, and habits don’t. The energy bar is a game simplification of that. Likewise, the meditation bonus, the chemistry tags and all learning speeds are simplified. A real brain has around 86 billion neurons; each orb here stands for an entire brain region.
